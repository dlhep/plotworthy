Converting a house into an HMO: the UK planning & licensing guide

Thinking of turning a house into a shared home for multiple tenants? Here's how the use classes, permitted development, Article 4 directions and licensing actually work — and what determines whether it stacks up.

Written by the Plotworthy planning & development team · Updated August 2026 · ~7 min read

Houses in Multiple Occupation (HMOs) can significantly boost rental yield — but "just renting rooms out" can quietly cross two separate lines at once: planning and licensing. Get ahead of both and an HMO can be a strong play; ignore them and it can go wrong fast. Here's the plain-English version.

The short version

  • A small HMO (3–6 unrelated people sharing) is Use Class C4. A large HMO (7+ sharers) is sui generis — its own category.
  • Nationally, changing from a house (C3) to a small HMO (C4) is often permitted development — but many councils remove this with an Article 4 direction, so you must check your area.
  • A large (sui generis) HMO always needs full planning permission.
  • Most HMOs also need an HMO licence and must meet minimum room sizes, amenities and fire standards — separate from planning.

What counts as an HMO?

Broadly, a property is an HMO when at least three tenants live there forming more than one household (i.e. not a single family) and share a kitchen, bathroom or toilet. The exact tests matter, so it's worth confirming your specific set-up, but that's the everyday definition most conversions are judged against.

Use classes: C4 vs sui generis

A small HMO for 3–6 sharers falls within Use Class C4. Once you go to 7 or more sharers, it becomes "sui generis" — literally "of its own kind" — which sits outside the standard classes and always requires its own planning permission. This threshold is one of the most important numbers in HMO planning.

Do you need planning permission?

For a small HMO (C3 to C4), national permitted development rights often allow the change without a full planning application. The catch: a large number of councils — especially in university towns and cities with HMO pressure — have made an Article 4 direction that removes this right in some or all of their area. Where an Article 4 applies, you need full planning permission even for a small HMO.

Always check for an Article 4 direction first. It's the single factor that most often decides whether your HMO is a simple conversion or a full planning application. Your local planning authority publishes where these apply.

For a large HMO (sui generis), there is no shortcut — a full planning application is always required, and councils will scrutinise parking, amenity, refuse storage and the concentration of HMOs in the street.

HMO licensing (separate from planning)

Planning permission is only half the picture. Most HMOs also need a licence. Mandatory licensing applies to HMOs occupied by five or more people forming two or more households, anywhere in England. On top of that, individual councils run additional and selective licensing schemes that can catch smaller HMOs too — so check your council's rules. Licensing brings its own standards and inspections.

Standards you'll need to meet

  • Minimum room sizes. Licensed HMOs have national minimum bedroom sizes (for example, floor areas below roughly 6.5 m² generally can't be used as sleeping accommodation for an adult). Undersized rooms can't count.
  • Amenities. Adequate kitchen, bathroom and toilet provision for the number of occupants.
  • Fire safety. Fire doors, alarms and escape routes appropriate to an HMO — often the biggest works item.
  • Management standards. HMO management regulations set duties for the person in control.

Costs and value

Conversion costs are driven mainly by fire works, additional bathrooms/kitchens and any layout changes to hit room standards. Against that, a well-run HMO typically produces a materially higher rental yield than a single-family let, which is why investors pursue them — but the uplift only holds if planning, licensing and standards are all satisfied.

The step-by-step process

  1. Check for an Article 4 direction covering your property.
  2. Confirm the use class — small (C4) or large (sui generis) — based on planned occupancy.
  3. Establish the planning route (permitted development, prior approval, or full application).
  4. Check the licensing regime — mandatory, additional or selective — for your council.
  5. Design to room-size, amenity and fire standards from the outset.
  6. Budget the works and model the yield before committing.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming C3→C4 is always permitted development and missing an Article 4 direction.
  • Creating a 7-bed HMO without realising it's sui generis and needs planning permission.
  • Letting rooms that are below the minimum licensed size.
  • Treating licensing and planning as the same thing — they're separate approvals.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need planning permission to turn my house into an HMO?

For a small HMO (3–6 sharers, Use Class C4), the change is often permitted development — unless your council has an Article 4 direction removing that right, in which case you need full planning permission. A large HMO (7+ sharers) is sui generis and always needs planning permission.

What is an Article 4 direction?

It's a step a council can take to remove permitted development rights in some or all of its area — commonly used to require planning permission for small HMOs. Where one applies, you can't rely on permitted development.

Do all HMOs need a licence?

Mandatory licensing applies to HMOs with five or more occupants in two or more households across England. Many councils also run additional or selective licensing that can catch smaller HMOs, so always check locally.

What's the minimum bedroom size in a licensed HMO?

National standards set minimum floor areas for sleeping rooms in licensed HMOs — rooms below roughly 6.5 m² generally can't be used as an adult bedroom. Your council can confirm the exact figures.
