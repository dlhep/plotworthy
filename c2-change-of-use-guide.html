Can I turn my house into a care home? A UK guide to C2 change of use

Thinking of converting a property into a care home, children's home or supported-living scheme? Here's what's actually possible, what the planning system requires, and how to work out whether it stacks up.

Written by the Plotworthy planning & development team · Updated August 2026 · ~8 min read

Converting a family home into a small care home or children's home is one of the most in-demand property plays in the UK right now — the returns can be strong and the social need is real. But it's also one of the most misunderstood, because it almost always turns on a single question: will you get planning permission? This guide walks you through it in plain English.

The short version

  • A care home, nursing home or larger children's home is a C2 "residential institution" use.
  • Changing a normal home (use class C3) to C2 always needs full planning permission — there's no permitted-development shortcut.
  • You'll also need to satisfy care regulation (CQC in England, or Ofsted for children's homes) — a separate process from planning.
  • Planners focus on parking, highways, neighbour amenity and design. Get ahead of these and your odds improve dramatically.

What is Use Class C2?

Under the Town and Country Planning (Use Classes) Order, Use Class C2 — "residential institutions" covers residential accommodation with care. That includes care homes, nursing homes, larger children's homes, hospitals, and some supported-living and residential-training settings. A standard dwelling — the house most people live in — is a different use class, C3.

Because C2 and C3 are separate use classes, turning a home into a care setting is a "material change of use" in the eyes of the planning system, and that's what triggers the need for permission.

Do you need planning permission to change to C2?

Yes — almost always, and it must be a full planning application. Unlike some commercial-to-residential conversions, there is no permitted-development right that lets you switch to C2 automatically. You can't rely on a quick "prior approval" route. This isn't a reason to be discouraged — care schemes are consented all the time — but it does mean the scheme has to be designed to satisfy the council's planning policies from the outset.

Two separate approvals, not one. A care home needs (1) planning permission for the change of use, and separately (2) registration with the regulator — the Care Quality Commission (CQC) for adult care in England, or Ofsted for children's homes. They're judged by different bodies against different rules, so plan for both from day one.

The C2 vs C3 grey area (especially children's homes)

Here's a nuance that trips people up. Not every care-type use is automatically C2. A small children's home run like an ordinary household — a few children living together as a family unit with carers — can sometimes fall within C3 (a dwellinghouse) rather than C2, and some supported-living arrangements sit in genuinely contested territory. Recent court cases have shown the boundary is fact-specific and legally unsettled.

Why does this matter? Because the use class affects whether you need permission at all, what conditions apply, and your flexibility later. It's exactly the kind of point where getting it wrong is expensive — and where early professional advice pays for itself.

What planners actually look at

When a council assesses a C2 application, the recurring themes are:

  • Parking & highways. Care uses generate staff shifts, visitors and deliveries. Expect to provide a parking or transport statement; good off-street provision helps enormously.
  • Neighbour amenity. Noise, comings and goings, and the character of a residential street are common objection points. Thoughtful design and community engagement reduce the risk.
  • Design & the building. Room sizes, accessibility, outdoor amenity space and fire strategy all shape the scheme — and they overlap with the regulator's standards.
  • Local planning policy. Some areas actively support care provision; others have specific policies or "Article 4" restrictions. The local plan is where the answer lives.

Costs and value — the rough shape

Every property is different, but as a guide, converting a substantial house to a small care use typically involves creating en-suite rooms, communal areas, and accessibility and fire works — often a six-figure spend once professional fees, planning and a contingency are included. Against that, a consented and operating C2 property can be worth considerably more than the same building as a family home, because it's valued on its income potential.

The honest catch: that uplift only lands if planning and registration both succeed. That's why the single smartest early move is to spend a small amount de-risking the planning position before you commit — ideally a pre-application enquiry with the council.

The step-by-step process

  1. Test feasibility. Establish what's realistically possible for your specific property and location before spending real money.
  2. Pre-application enquiry. Get the council's informal view on a C2 change of use — the highest-value de-risking step, especially before a purchase.
  3. Appoint an architect. To develop a scheme that answers parking, amenity and care standards, and to run the planning application.
  4. Scope regulation early. Design room sizes, access and fire strategy to CQC/Ofsted standards from the start, not as a retrofit.
  5. Submit the planning application with the supporting statements the council expects.
  6. Line up a builder to price the works once designs are progressing.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Buying the property before checking the planning position with the council.
  • Assuming a permitted-development shortcut exists for C2 — it doesn't.
  • Designing for planning but ignoring CQC/Ofsted standards (or vice versa).
  • Under-providing parking on a residential street.
  • Getting the C2-vs-C3 classification wrong and building on a shaky legal footing.

Frequently asked questions

Can I turn my house into a care home without planning permission?

No. Changing from a dwelling (C3) to a care home (C2) is a material change of use that requires full planning permission. There is no permitted-development right that allows it automatically.

Is a children's home always Class C2?

Not necessarily. A small children's home run like an ordinary household can, in some cases, fall within C3 rather than C2, while larger homes are C2. The boundary is fact-specific and legally nuanced, so it's worth confirming with a planning professional.

Do I need CQC registration as well as planning permission?

Yes — they're separate. Planning permission covers the change of use; the Care Quality Commission (for adult care in England) or Ofsted (for children's homes) regulates the operation of the service. You need both.

How long does a C2 planning application take?

A typical planning application runs on the order of a couple of months for a decision once submitted, but timescales vary by council and scheme. A pre-application enquiry beforehand adds time up front but reduces risk.

Will converting to C2 add value to my property?

Often, yes — a consented, operating care use is valued on its income potential and can be worth considerably more than the same building as a home. But the uplift depends on securing both planning permission and registration, so it isn't guaranteed.
