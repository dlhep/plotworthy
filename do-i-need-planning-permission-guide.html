Do I need planning permission? A UK homeowner's guide

Some projects need a full planning application; many don't. Here's how to tell the difference — permitted development, prior approval, the exceptions that catch people out, and how to be certain.

Written by the Plotworthy planning & development team · Updated August 2026 · ~7 min read

"Do I need planning permission?" is the first question almost every property project runs into — and the honest answer is "it depends." The good news: there's a logical way to work it out, and a large share of home improvements fall under permitted development, meaning no full application is needed.

The short version

  • Planning permission is formal consent from your council for development. A full application is the standard route.
  • Permitted development (PD) lets you carry out many projects without a full application, within set limits.
  • PD rights can be removed by an Article 4 direction, and are restricted for flats, listed buildings and conservation areas.
  • A change of use (e.g. house to care home, HMO or flats) usually needs permission — this is where value-adding projects most often sit.

Planning permission vs permitted development

Think of it as two routes. Planning permission means submitting a full application that the council assesses against its policies before deciding. Permitted development is a set of nationally-granted rights that let you do certain things automatically, provided you stay within the limits (on size, height, position, materials and so on). Many extensions, loft conversions and outbuildings fall under PD — but the limits are precise, and exceeding them by even a little pushes you back into needing permission.

Projects that often don't need a full application

Subject to the limits and the exceptions below, these are commonly permitted development for houses:

  • Many single-storey rear extensions within depth, height and coverage limits.
  • Loft conversions within volume limits (and usually without a balcony or raised platform).
  • Outbuildings, garden rooms and garages within size and siting rules.
  • Some porches, and minor alterations, within limits.
The limits are the whole game. Permitted development is generous but exact — depths, heights, proportion of garden covered, distance from boundaries and materials all matter. A design that's just over a limit needs planning permission.

Projects that usually do need permission

  • Change of use — turning a house into a care home (C2), an HMO, flats, or commercial use. These are where the biggest value uplift usually is, and they almost always need permission.
  • Larger or two-storey extensions beyond PD limits.
  • New dwellings or building on a separate plot.
  • Most works to the front of a house or facing a highway.

The exceptions that catch people out

  • Article 4 directions. A council can remove PD rights in a defined area — common for HMOs and in conservation areas. Where one applies, you need permission even for something normally permitted.
  • Flats and maisonettes. Most householder PD rights apply to houses, not flats — so a flat usually needs permission for works a house wouldn't.
  • Listed buildings. Listed building consent is a separate, stricter regime; even internal changes can need consent.
  • Conservation areas & other designations. PD rights are restricted, and extra controls can apply.

What is "prior approval"?

Some permitted development is subject to a prior approval process — a lighter-touch check where the council confirms specific matters (like impact on neighbours or highways) before you proceed. It's not a full application, but it isn't automatic either. Larger single-storey rear extensions and certain change-of-use routes use this process.

How to be certain: a Lawful Development Certificate

If you believe a project is permitted development but want proof — for peace of mind, or for a future sale — you can apply for a Lawful Development Certificate (LDC). It's the council formally confirming your project is lawful without needing planning permission. Many owners get one before relying on PD for anything significant.

A simple way to work it out

  1. Is it a change of use? If yes, assume you need permission.
  2. Is the property a flat, listed, or in a conservation area? If yes, PD is restricted — check carefully.
  3. Is there an Article 4 direction? If yes, PD rights may be removed.
  4. Does the project fit within PD limits? If yes, it may be permitted — consider a Lawful Development Certificate to be sure.
  5. If in doubt, ask the council (a pre-application enquiry) or get professional advice before you spend.

Frequently asked questions

Does an extension always need planning permission?

No. Many single-storey rear extensions and loft conversions fall under permitted development if they stay within the size, height and siting limits. Larger or two-storey extensions, and works to the front, usually need permission.

Do I need planning permission to change the use of my property?

Usually yes. A material change of use — such as a house to a care home, HMO or flats — normally requires planning permission, and there's rarely a permitted-development shortcut.

What is an Article 4 direction?

It's a step a council can take to remove permitted development rights in a defined area, meaning you need planning permission for something that would normally be permitted.

How can I be sure I don't need permission?

Apply for a Lawful Development Certificate — the council formally confirms your project is lawful without planning permission. It's the safest way to rely on permitted development.
