Converting a house into flats: the UK guide

Splitting a house into two or more flats can unlock real value — but it almost always needs planning permission and has to meet space, amenity and building standards. Here's what's involved.

Written by the Plotworthy planning & development team · Updated August 2026 · ~7 min read

A larger house — especially one that's already been divided in the past, or has an obvious layout to split — can often be worth more as two or three flats than as a single home. But unlike some conversions, this one is firmly a planning exercise, and the detail is where schemes are won or lost.

The short version

  • Converting a single house into flats needs full planning permission — there is no permitted-development shortcut for splitting a house into self-contained flats.
  • Each flat usually needs to meet minimum space standards and have adequate light, outlook and amenity.
  • Parking, cycle storage, refuse/bin storage and privacy are common council concerns.
  • Building Regulations — especially sound insulation and fire separation between flats — apply alongside planning.

Do you need planning permission?

Yes. Creating self-contained flats from a single dwelling is a material change of use and needs a full planning application. (This is different from commercial-to-residential conversions, some of which have permitted-development routes — a house-to-flats split does not.) The council assesses whether the number and size of units suit the property and the street.

What planners look at

  • Unit sizes & layout. Each flat should meet minimum internal space standards (many councils apply the Nationally Described Space Standard) and provide proper light and outlook — no windowless bedrooms or cramped studios.
  • Amenity. Private or shared outdoor space, bin and cycle storage, and avoiding overlooking or noise problems for neighbours and between flats.
  • Parking & highways. More units usually means more parking demand; provision (or a good case for low-car locations) matters.
  • External changes. New or altered entrances, windows, or extensions to make the split work all form part of the assessment, and can raise design and neighbour issues.

Building Regulations (separate from planning)

Planning decides whether you can create the flats; Building Regulations govern how they're built. The big ones for conversions are sound insulation between dwellings (Part E), fire separation and escape (Part B), plus ventilation, thermal and electrical standards. Retrofitting proper sound and fire separation into an existing house is often the largest single cost.

Leasehold & title: if the property is leasehold, or you plan to sell the flats on long leases, get legal advice early — the ownership structure affects both feasibility and value.

Costs and value

The main cost drivers are the works needed to create genuinely separate, compliant units: sound and fire separation, additional kitchens and bathrooms, separate entrances, and utility metering. Against that, the combined value of well-designed flats can exceed the value of the house as a single dwelling — the classic reason to convert — but the margin depends on unit sizes, location and the local flat market, so the numbers need testing before you commit.

The step-by-step process

  1. Test feasibility — how many flats the property could realistically yield to standard.
  2. Consider a pre-application enquiry to gauge the council's view on unit numbers and parking.
  3. Appoint an architect to design compliant units and run the planning application.
  4. Plan for Building Regulations — sound, fire and ventilation — from the design stage.
  5. Model the numbers — combined flat values vs works, fees and finance.
  6. Get builder quotes once the design is progressing.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a house-to-flats split is permitted development — it isn't.
  • Squeezing in too many undersized units; quality and space standards protect value and consent.
  • Under-budgeting sound and fire separation works.
  • Ignoring parking, bins and cycle storage — small details that sink applications.

Frequently asked questions

Can I convert my house into flats without planning permission?

No. Splitting a single house into self-contained flats is a material change of use and requires full planning permission. There is no permitted-development shortcut for it.

How many flats can I create?

It depends on the size and layout of the property, the space standards each flat must meet, and local factors like parking and amenity. A feasibility assessment establishes a realistic number for your specific property.

Do Building Regulations apply as well as planning?

Yes — they're separate. Planning decides whether the flats are acceptable in principle; Building Regulations govern how they're built, particularly sound insulation and fire separation between the flats.

Will converting to flats add value?

Often the combined value of well-designed flats exceeds the value of the house as a single home, which is why people convert — but the margin depends on unit sizes, location and the local market, so the numbers should be modelled before committing.
